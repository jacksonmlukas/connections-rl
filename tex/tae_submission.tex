\title{Reliable and Invalid: Entropy Collapse Separates a Verifiable Reward
  from the Construct It Proxies}
\workshoptitle{TAE (Trust-AI-Eval): Can We Trust AI Evaluation?}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Verifiable rewards are trusted because they are deterministic: a program
either awards the points or it does not. We post-train Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
with GRPO on NYT Connections under a reward with a cheap structural component
(well-formed output) and an expensive semantic component (correct
groupings). On a strictly chronological test set of 162 puzzles (648 group
slots), the step-50 policy recovers 77 groups against the untrained base
model's 26 --- a paired improvement of $+0.315$ $[+0.204, +0.438]$ on the
0--4 scale. By step 403 the same run recovers 4, below base by $-0.136$
$[-0.191, -0.080]$, reproduced across three seeds. The \emph{training}
reward records none of this: it reaches the reward's ceiling of 1.6 with
zero across-rollout variance by step 125 and stays there, while the
identical reward evaluated out of sample tracks the whole arc, peaking at
0.246 at step 50 and ending at 0.125 against base's 0.165. The semantic
peak arrives at 5.7\% of the run's eventual KL displacement; the remainder
undoes it as policy entropy collapses and structural validity climbs
monotonically. The collapse survives disabling TRL's group-level reward
normalization, and three LLM judges scoring the test generations blind all
rank step 50 above base above the final policy. At 1.5B, with almost no
semantic capability to trade, the same recipe leaves the construct exactly
where it found it --- one recovered group of 648 in each arm. We read this
as a construct-validity failure: the in-sample metric cannot locate the
point at which a verifiable reward stops helping; the same reward, held
out, locates it exactly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Post-training against verifiable rewards is attractive for a reason that
sounds decisive: the reward is a program. It has no parameters to
misgeneralize, no preferences to drift, and it returns exactly what it was
written to return at every point in policy space. The argument that this
buys immunity from reward hacking\footnote{We follow the common usage
\emph{reward hacking} throughout; \citet{skalse2022}, whose formalization we
adopt, publish it under the name \emph{reward gaming}.} rests on the reward's
reliability. This paper is about the gap that reliability does not close: the
gap between the reward and the construct it stands in for.

We train on NYT Connections --- sixteen words, to be partitioned into four
groups of four --- chosen because well-formedness and correctness are
unusually separable: a response can be a perfectly valid partition of the
board and completely wrong. Our reward pays for both, asymmetrically. Two of
its five components can be checked by reading the string; three require the
answer key.

For fifty steps it works. Held-out groups-correct nearly triples against the
untrained base model, from 0.160 to 0.475 --- 26 recovered group slots of 648
to 77 --- and clears the supervised checkpoint the run is initialized from,
which sits at 0.346 and 56 slots. Held-out mean reward reaches the highest
value we measure anywhere in this study. Over the following 353 steps the
same run drives that capability down to 0.025, four recovered slots, below
the untrained base model and far below the supervised checkpoint it started
from, while the training reward saturates at its ceiling and the
invalid-output rate falls by an order of magnitude.

The training signal does not merely miss this reversal --- it reports its
opposite. From step 125 onward the logged training reward sits at 1.6, the
maximum the reward function can emit, with zero variance across the $K=8$
rollouts on nearly every prompt. A practitioner watching that curve saw a
solved task. The same reward function, evaluated on data the policy was not
updated against, ranks all three checkpoints correctly. The failure is not
in the metric; it is in the universal practice of reading it in sample.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (1)~A fully verifiable, temporally
leakage-controlled case study in which GRPO against a partially verifiable
reward first triples and then destroys the target construct, ending below
the untrained base model, reproduced across three seeds
(\S\ref{sec:arc}). (2)~A checkpoint-level account locating the semantic
peak at 5.7\% of the run's eventual KL displacement and identifying entropy
collapse as the mechanism, with the reward's cheap component climbing
monotonically throughout (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}). (3)~Evidence that the
training-side signal is structurally incapable of recording the reversal:
the training reward reaches its ceiling with zero rollout variance by step
125, at which point the group-relative advantage is identically zero on
$\sim$95\% of prompts (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}). (4)~An ablation
disabling TRL's group-level reward normalization --- the confound three of
four adversarial reviewers of an earlier draft independently named --- which
reproduces the hump, the collapse, and the below-base endpoint
(\S\ref{sec:ablation}). (5)~A blind LLM-judge evaluation showing that
external scorers with no answer key rank the checkpoints correctly, i.e.,
the reversal is legible from outside the reward
(\S\ref{sec:judge}). (6)~A scale contrast at 1.5B where the same recipe
optimizes only what it can verify and leaves the (absent) construct
untouched (\S\ref{sec:scale}).

\section{Task, pipeline, and the reward's asymmetry}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Task and data.} 1{,}078 dated NYT Connections puzzles (1{,}077
after validation), split strictly chronologically: 807 train (2023-06-12 to
2025-08-27), 108 validation, 162 test (2025-12-15 to 2026-05-29). Every test
puzzle postdates every training puzzle; regressing groups-correct on puzzle
date across the test window yields slopes indistinguishable from zero for
both the base model ($-0.158$ groups/year $[-0.634, +0.292]$) and the trained
policy ($+0.035$ $[-0.271, +0.329]$), so the split controls contamination
without introducing measurable drift.

\paragraph{Pipeline.} Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (and 1.5B for the scale contrast),
rank-16 LoRA. SFT for 3 epochs on the training split, then GRPO
\citep{deepseekmath} from the SFT checkpoint: $K=8$ sampled completions per
puzzle at temperature 0.9, group-relative advantages, $\beta=0.001$ against
the SFT reference, learning rate $5\times10^{-6}$, one epoch (403 optimizer
steps) at 7B. All training ran on free-tier T4 GPUs.

\paragraph{The reward.} Table~\ref{tab:reward} is the crux of the paper.

Maximum 1.6. The model can raise its score two ways: by getting better at
the puzzle, or by getting better at emitting well-formed output. Only one of
those is the construct. That asymmetry --- a cheap component and an
expensive component, independently satisfiable --- is the design property
this paper studies.

The method works, then reverses. At step 50 GRPO has nearly tripled the base
model's semantic performance, 26 recovered group slots to 77, and the paired
interval excludes zero ($+0.315$ $[+0.204, +0.438]$). By step 403 the same
run sits at 4 slots, below the untrained base model ($-0.136$
$[-0.191, -0.080]$) and far below the supervised checkpoint it was
initialized from, which recovers 56 slots at 0.346 (served in a separate
session; no paired interval attached). Means in Table~\ref{tab:arc} are
exact ratios of the count column, and we report both because the
multiplicative language used throughout this paper is describing the
difference between 26, 77 and 4 slots out of 648. The question is not
whether verifiable-reward RL helps. It is why nothing in the
\emph{training} signal says when it has stopped.

Two details make the mechanism visible rather than inferred. Held-out mean
reward peaks at step 50 at 0.246, the highest value of any arm we measure,
and ends at 0.125, below base's 0.165, while the training reward reaches the
reward function's ceiling by step 125 (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}). The same
reward function ranks the three checkpoints correctly when it is evaluated
out of sample and fails to when it is read off the training curve; that
contrast, and not the reward's determinism, is this paper's subject. And the
step-50 policy is \emph{worse than base on structure}, 16.7\% invalid
against 6.8\%. It is \emph{not} worse than the checkpoint it was actually
initialized from, since the SFT arm is 22.2\% invalid --- measured against
its own starting point, step 50 has improved on both axes at once. This
constrains how the trajectory may be described: what is specific to the
phase after step 50 is the semantic collapse, not the structural climb,
which was already more than half complete before the peak
(\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).

\paragraph{The peak is a plateau, not a knife edge.} Step 100 of the same
run was additionally evaluated on test, in a single later serving session
alongside a re-serve of step 50: 76/648 groups recovered (0.4691
$[0.3519, 0.6049]$) against step 50's 80/648 (0.4938 $[0.3827, 0.6235]$)
\emph{in that session}, paired difference $-0.0247$ $[-0.1481, +0.0927]$
($n=162$, seed-0 bootstrap) --- statistically indistinguishable, with step
100 recording three full solves to step 50's two. The peak spans at least
steps 50--100; the collapse occurs between steps 100 and 150, where the
validation semantic score falls from 35/400 to 3/400 on the $n=100$
temperature-0.9 entropy sweep (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}). The step-50
selection therefore does not rest on a one-column argmax.

\paragraph{Reproducibility across serving sessions.} Base and the final
checkpoint reproduce per-puzzle \emph{exactly} across three independent
vLLM sessions (0 of 162 differences each). The step-50 checkpoint does not:
it differed on 2/162 puzzles across sessions (77 vs.\ 80 of 648) and on
6/162 completions between two greedy passes within one session.
Mid-training policies sit near argmax ties that batched inference resolves
inconsistently; converged ones have none left. That is evidence \emph{for}
the entropy account, and it is why every step-50 number in this paper
carries its serving session. The published headline (77/648) is the
original single-session evaluation; the plateau comparison above uses the
later session's within-session pair.

\paragraph{Robustness of the endpoint.} Three independent 7B seeds land at
4, 7 and 11 recovered slots of 648 ($0.045 \pm 0.022$ on the 0--4 scale) ---
all below base's 26. Their LoRA update directions agree pairwise (cosine
$+0.67$ to $+0.69$ against a random-pair expectation near $10^{-5}$):
different random draws found the same attractor. Best-of-16 sampling does
not rescue the converged policy --- pass@16 differences of $+0.920$
$[0.778, 1.062]$ for SFT over GRPO and $-0.370$ $[-0.475, -0.259]$ for GRPO
against base --- which is what a collapsed-entropy account predicts: a
near-deterministic model has nothing new to sample. These pass@16 numbers
are single-seed and we read them as illustrative.

\paragraph{Conditioning on well-formed output widens the gap rather than
explaining it, and we label the operation for what it is.} A reviewer will
ask whether the effect is an artifact of validity, since invalid generations
score zero groups, and validity is what GRPO improved. Restricting to
structurally valid generations at 7B: base \textbf{0.172} ($n=151$ of 162),
SFT \textbf{0.444} ($n=126$), GRPO \textbf{0.025} ($n=161$). This is a
rescaling and we label it as one: each conditional value is that arm's
unconditional mean divided by its validity rate, exactly, so the gap widens
automatically whenever the low-scoring arm is the more valid one, which here
it is by construction. What the rescaling licenses is therefore only the
negative claim, which is the claim we need --- the deficit is not an
artifact of formatting, because it survives with formatting held fixed.
Among answers that parse, the converged policy recovers 4 group slots where
the untrained model recovers 26. At 1.5B: base 0.009 ($n=110$), SFT 0.048
($n=42$), GRPO 0.006 ($n=158$), on underlying counts of 1, 2 and 1.

\paragraph{One prediction of our own account fails, and we report it.} If
entropy collapse selectively destroyed board-specific inference, the loss
should concentrate in the strata that need the most inference. It does not:
relative loss from base to GRPO runs 92\% on \texttt{category} ($n=69$),
67\% on \texttt{cultural} ($n=28$), 86\% on \texttt{tag-fillin} ($n=40$) and
100\% on \texttt{wordplay} ($n=21$, from a near-floor base of 0.048). That
is closer to uniform destruction than to a gradient, which rules out the
selective-inference story without touching the entropy one.

\section{The scale contrast at 1.5B}
\label{sec:scale}

At this scale the counts are the honest reading (full table in
Appendix~\ref{app:small}). Base recovers one group slot of 648, GRPO
recovers one, and SFT --- the best arm --- recovers two; agreement ``to
five decimal places'' between base and GRPO is two integers that both equal
1, and no interval arithmetic on numbers this small should be read as
meaning more than that. The reward did at 1.5B exactly what it did at 7B
--- it optimized the component it can verify, driving invalid output from
32.1\% to 2.5\% and mean reward from 0.049 to 0.113. What differs between
scales is how much unverifiable ability existed to be displaced. At 1.5B
there was essentially none, so nothing was lost. At 7B there was real
ability, and it went.

Two caveats attach. First, the scales are epoch-asymmetric: the 1.5B run
trained for 806 optimizer steps --- two epochs over the 807 training puzzles
--- versus 403 steps, one epoch, at 7B. Second, and by our own argument the
more important one: we have no 1.5B checkpoint trajectory, so ``the
construct was untouched at 1.5B'' is a claim about the 1.5B endpoint and
nothing more --- and an endpoint is exactly what this paper shows cannot
settle such a question. The claim that survives without a trajectory is the
weaker one: whatever happened at 1.5B left no residue at the endpoint, on a
base model with one recoverable group of 648 to begin with.

\section{Mechanism: entropy collapse and the step-50 semantic peak}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} scores checkpoints of the reported run on the
$n=100$ temperature-0.9 entropy sweep --- 100 validation puzzles, sampled
decoding, the same forward passes used for the entropy and KL measurements
(full per-checkpoint values in Appendix~\ref{app:ckpt}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../figs/fig1_entropy_collapse.pdf}
\caption{The mechanism, on the $n=100$ temperature-0.9 entropy sweep
(reported 7B run, seed 0). \textbf{Left:} policy entropy against optimizer
step --- a 30-fold collapse, with the cliff between steps 100 and 150.
\textbf{Right:} semantic score against KL from the SFT initialization, the
over-optimization curve: the peak arrives at 2.70 of an eventual 47.05 nats
per sequence (5.7\% of the run's displacement), and the remaining 94.3\%
undoes it. The untrained base model is shown as a reference; it is not on
the GRPO trajectory.}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}
(Throughout this paper: the \emph{entropy sweep} is $n=100$ at temperature
0.9, denominators of 400; the ablation's \emph{checkpoint sweep} in
\S\ref{sec:ablation} is $n=108$, greedy, denominators of 432. The two are
different measurements and are never mixed.)

The semantic peak has a location; the structural climb does not. The
semantic score rises from 0.0325 at the SFT initialization to 0.0950 at step
50 (13/400 to 38/400 recovered slots), then falls to 0.0075 by step 150 and
never recovers. The structural score --- the fraction of outputs that are a
valid partition, not shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} --- climbs
monotonically across the same window and past it: 0.37 at the SFT
initialization, 0.68 at step 50, 0.95 at step 150, 0.96 thereafter. Of the
0.59 total structural gain, 0.31 is already banked by step 50, concurrent
with the semantic peak, and 0.28 arrives afterward.

The trajectory is therefore not a trade with a turning point at which
structure begins to be bought and semantics begins to be sold. It is a
monotone structural climb with a semantic hump on top of it, and only the
hump has a location. The entropy account survives this reading unchanged ---
it predicts a policy that ends reliably well-formed and adaptively wrong,
which is what the endpoint is --- but it does not license the vocabulary of
a trade and we do not use it.

\paragraph{Entropy is the mechanism.} Policy entropy falls 30-fold from the
SFT initialization to the end of the run, 13.4-fold between steps 50 and
150 alone, the window in which the semantic score falls 12.7-fold. By step
150 the policy is effectively deterministic: it produces nearly the same
kind of output regardless of which sixteen words it is handed. A
near-deterministic policy can be reliably well-formed --- the format is the
same for every board and can be satisfied without reading the puzzle. It
cannot be adaptively correct, because correctness requires responding to
the specific sixteen words in front of it, which requires variation in the
output. Collapse the variation and what remains is the component of the
reward that does not depend on the input.

\paragraph{KL is the budget view.} The step-50 checkpoint sits 2.70 nats
from the SFT initialization and is the best policy this run produced on
held-out data by a wide margin. The run spent 47.05. Stopping at 5.7\% of
the eventual KL displacement would have returned a model that recovers 77
group slots against base's 26; running to convergence returned one that
recovers 4.

\paragraph{The training signal, read from the trainer's own logs.} Figure
\ref{fig:rewards} shows the paper's central contrast on one axis: the
training reward reaches the reward function's ceiling --- 1.6000, with
standard deviation 0.0000 across the $K=8$ rollouts --- by step 125 and
stays there. What the figure cannot show is TRL's own
\texttt{frac\_reward\_zero\_std} diagnostic: the fraction of prompt groups
whose eight sampled rewards are identical, i.e., whose group-relative
advantage is exactly zero. It rises from 0.0 at step 50 to 1.0 at step 125
and averages 0.946 over the remainder of training, so from step 125 onward
the optimizer receives no learning signal on nearly every prompt. What
drives the remaining 278 steps of movement while $\sim$95\% of prompts
carry zero advantage, we report as an open question; plausibly the residual
prompts that still carry signal, compounded, but we have not measured that
causal chain and do not assert it.

\paragraph{$\beta$ was numerically inert, and the bracket is not an
experiment.} An earlier configuration at learning rate $10^{-6}$ and
$\beta=0.04$ froze the policy (KL $\approx 0.001$ for 403 steps; greedy
outputs byte-identical to SFT). The reported configuration, at
$5\times10^{-6}$ and $\beta=0.001$, overshoots by roughly as much as the
first undershoots. We state two qualifications immediately, because both
weaken the pair as an experiment and neither weakens the observation.
First, the two configurations differ in \emph{two} variables at once,
$\beta$ by $40\times$ and the learning rate by $5\times$; a policy frozen
at the lower learning rate is the expected consequence of the learning rate
alone, so the pair bounds a region jointly and attributes nothing to either
variable individually, and we do not claim to have measured inside a KL
window. Second, $\beta=0.001$ was numerically inert throughout the
reported run: at step 50, $\beta \cdot \mathrm{KL} = 0.001 \times 2.70 =
0.0027$ reward units --- eighteen times smaller than the one-away
partial-credit term and 0.17\% of the 1.6 maximum --- and at convergence it
reaches 0.047. The KL reference never restrained the policy at any point
in the reported run, so this paper contains no evidence about what a KL
penalty does when it is large enough to matter, and the
intermediate-$\beta$ sweep remains unrun.

\section{The ablation: the collapse is not the normalization default}
\label{sec:ablation}

We inherited \texttt{scale\_rewards="group"} from TRL --- verified as the
default in trl 1.10.0, whose \texttt{GRPOConfig.\_\_post\_init\_\_}
normalizes \texttt{False} to \texttt{"none"}. Three of four adversarial
reviewers of an earlier draft independently identified it as a confound:
once the policy is near-deterministic, the within-group standard deviation
that the advantage is divided by approaches zero, amplifying sampling noise
into large updates, and that alone might produce the collapse, its timing,
and the cross-seed agreement. \citet{drgrpo} argue against this
normalization on independent grounds.

We reran the 7B pipeline identically --- same SFT warm start, learning
rate, $\beta$, $K$, temperature, LoRA rank, epoch count, seed --- with
reward scaling disabled, the resolved value read back off the constructed
trainer configuration and logged. The signature is unchanged on the greedy
$n=108$ checkpoint sweep: validation semantic score rises to 62/432 at step
50, falls to 35/432 by step 100 and 4/432 by step 150, while structural
validity climbs 0.70 to 0.96. The validation-selected peak (step 50, frozen
before any test puzzle was scored) recovers 87/648 on test (0.5370
$[0.4134, 0.6668]$, three solves), $+0.377$ $[+0.247, +0.506]$ paired
against base. The final policy recovers 7/648 (0.0432 $[0.0123, 0.0804]$)
--- below base by $-0.117$ $[-0.179, -0.056]$ paired, and within $+0.019$
$[0.000, +0.043]$ of the original run's final policy.

The dynamics match as well: without reward scaling, policy entropy falls
0.2111 (step 50) $\to$ 0.1346 (100) $\to$ 0.0128 (150) and stays near 0.011
through step 403 (the original run's endpoint: 0.0099 nats/token), while KL
from the SFT initialization rises 2.75 $\to$ 17.84 $\to$ 45.64 nats per
sequence and plateaus near 46 (original: 47.05); on the $n=100$
temperature-0.9 entropy sweep --- a different measurement from the greedy
$n=108$ checkpoint sweep above --- semantic score reads 35/400 at step 50,
17/400 at 100, then 2/400 from step 150 onward. The hump, the collapse,
the entropy trajectory, and the below-base endpoint all survive with the
normalization off. The phenomenon is not a library default.

\section{The reversal is legible from outside the reward}
\label{sec:judge}

The 162 test generations from base, step 50 and the final policy were
scored blind --- board words and completion only, no answer key --- by
three LLM judges (gpt-5-mini, gemini-2.5-flash, claude-sonnet-4-5) on a
1--10 scale, 162 items per arm per judge, 1458/1458 responses parsed. All
three rank step 50 above base above the final policy, and all nine pairwise
per-puzzle differences exclude zero under a paired bootstrap (seed 0);
step-50 minus final is $+0.60$ $[+0.44, +0.78]$, $+3.25$ $[+2.81, +3.67]$
and $+1.62$ $[+1.33, +1.90]$ respectively. Every judge rates the collapsed
policy below the untrained base despite its near-perfect output format ---
though judges err in correlated ways \citep{kohli2026}, so we read this as
three correlated readings of one external signal rather than three
independent confirmations. A shared preference for fluent, well-formed text
would by itself produce this ranking; that is the finding rather than a
defect in it, because the property the judges are responding to is one the
training reward awards full marks to. Any external scorer that separates
step 50 from step 403 is reading something the reward cannot. The training
reward cannot record the reversal; a blind judge can.

\section{Implications for evaluation practice}
\label{sec:implications}

\paragraph{Distinguish in-sample from held-out proxy metrics before
concluding a proxy has failed.} Our reward does not fail as a ranking of
these three checkpoints. Evaluated on held-out data it places step 50 above
base above the final policy, which is the correct order. It fails only in
the form practitioners actually consult it: as a training curve, in sample,
where it reads perfect. The cheap remedy is not a better reward. It is the
same reward, run on data the policy was not updated against.

\paragraph{Report reward components separately, never the aggregate.} This
failure is invisible in the sum and obvious in the decomposition, and the
decomposition is already computed. Component decomposition is a standard
reward-model diagnostic; it is simply not applied to rewards that are
deterministic, because determinism is taken to have settled the question.

\paragraph{Treat single-seed RL results as uninterpretable.} Our own three
seeds span 0.025 to 0.068 at the endpoint. A single seed cannot tell you
where in that range you landed. This applies with equal force to results
that come out well, including the step-50 result in this paper, which is
one seed, selected as the argmax of a nine-point validation grid on which
step 100 scores 0.0875 against step 50's 0.0950 at $n=100$, a gap
comfortably inside one standard error. The validation sweep preceded every
test evaluation of the selected checkpoint --- the step-50 checkpoint was
first scored on test only after the grid existed, and the ablation's peak
was likewise frozen from validation before its test run --- so this is
ordinary validation-based selection; and the subsequent test evaluation of
step 100 (\S\ref{sec:arc}) shows the selection rests on a plateau rather
than a lucky column.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reward-model overoptimization.} \citet{gao2023scaling}
established this curve shape --- gold-standard performance rising and then
declining as KL from the initialization grows --- for \emph{learned} reward
models, where the accepted explanation is Goodhart against an
approximation, remediable in principle with a better reward model;
\citet{stiennon2020} report the same reversal. Our reward is a program: it
approximates nothing, and there is nothing in it to game. The curve
therefore requires only that a reward's cheap and expensive components be
independently satisfiable --- a property of the reward's design, not of its
fit --- which is a stronger reason for concern, because determinism is the
exact property cited when practitioners argue that verifiable rewards
escape this failure mode.

\paragraph{RL for reasoning and entropy collapse.} Verifiable-reward RL
underlies recent reasoning systems \citep{deepseekr1, deepseekmath}.
\citet{yue2025} question whether RLVR extends capability beyond the base
model's pass@$k$ ceiling; our result is complementary and sharper in one
respect --- here RLVR ends \emph{below} the base model on the construct.
\citet{cui2025} document entropy collapse as a central dynamic of RL for
reasoning; we observe the collapse co-occurring with the destruction of the
construct while the verifiable reward's cheap component continues to
improve. \citet{drgrpo} critique group-level advantage normalization; our
ablation (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) shows the phenomenon here does not depend
on it.

\paragraph{Reward hacking and its taxonomy.} \citet{skalse2022} formalize
the failure mode (under the name \emph{reward gaming});
\citet{abahana2026} classifies RLHF failure transitions by directional
changes in learned reward and external judge scores, and reports that
row-level diagnostics surface failures that checkpoint averages hide. Two
things differ here. Our reward is rule-based and verifiable rather than a
learned model, so the gap we report is not reward-model misgeneralization
but a gap between a rule that is correct and the construct that rule stands
in for. And our reversal is bracketed rather than flagged: the collapsed
policy scores below the untrained base on held-out data, which dates the
turn instead of detecting it.

\paragraph{Validity in AI evaluation.} Measurement-theoretic critiques of
ML evaluation \citep{jacobs2021, freiesleben2025} distinguish a measurement
from the construct it operationalizes; \citet{chehbouni2025} apply the
frame to LLM judges. Our contribution is a case where the measurement is
maximally reliable --- deterministic, verifiable, reproducible --- and the
validity gap does the damage anyway. \citet{agarwal2021} motivate the
statistical practice (paired bootstrap, counts alongside means, multi-seed
reporting) we follow.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

One task, one reward design, chosen because it separates form from content
unusually cleanly. The checkpoint trajectory is 7B seed 0 only, because
only that run was Hub-synced during training --- so the scale-dependence
claim rests on endpoints at both scales and a trajectory at one, and by our
own argument an endpoint cannot rule out a traversed-and-returned peak at
1.5B (\S\ref{sec:scale}); the 1.5B run also trained two epochs to the 7B's
one.

There is no human baseline. We do not know what groups-correct a competent
human scores on this test split. ``Worse than the untrained base model'' is
established. ``Bad in absolute terms'' is not a claim we are entitled to
make.

The headline checkpoint rests on one seed. Our three-seed evidence covers
the converged endpoint, not the peak, and the endpoint spread of 0.025 to
0.068 is the reason we distrust single numbers elsewhere in this paper. The
height and the location of the peak may both move across seeds and we have
measured neither, though the step-100 test evaluation shows the peak is at
least not a knife edge in training time.

We report a single $\beta$ alongside one that froze the policy, and no run
at an intermediate value; the two configurations differ in learning rate as
well as in $\beta$, and $\beta=0.001$ contributed at most 0.047 reward
units at any point in the reported run. Entropy regularization and a larger
KL penalty are untested recommendations, not findings.

The blind-judge evidence is three correlated scorers, not three independent
votes \citep{kohli2026}, and we claim only what correlated scorers can
support (\S\ref{sec:judge}). Training-reward saturation at the ceiling
implies the policy solves its training prompts under sampling from step 125
onward; whether that reflects SFT-stage memorization of the training
answers is a natural hypothesis that we have not measured --- neither SFT
nor the final policy has been evaluated offline on training puzzles --- and
we flag it as open rather than assert it.
